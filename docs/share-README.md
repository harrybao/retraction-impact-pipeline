# CS-43 — shared datasets

Stage 1 (page archiving, citation data, news and social attention, Wikipedia
across eleven language editions) and Stage 2 (the article text and the
retraction notice read out of each archive) are complete, and both are in this
folder. **Stage 3 starts from here.**

Everything here is generated by the pipeline in `scripts/`. Nothing is
hand-edited, and every file rebuilds from the database:

```
python scripts/clean_data.py            # cleaning + long-format tables
python scripts/verify_archives.py       # sets documents.usable
python scripts/flag_shared_bodies.py    # which bodies are not the paper's own
python scripts/export_tables.py         # per-host, per-publisher, altmetric, wikipedia
python scripts/export_master.py         # the joined master table
python scripts/export_missing.py        # every page we do not have, and why
python scripts/export_text.py           # Stage 2: index, notices, full text
python scripts/export_archive_index.py  # the key to the archive folder
python scripts/build_share.py           # assembles this folder
```

## Every page here was verified, not merely fetched

A capture counts as archived if the file **carries the DOI it is filed under,
or its title matches the record**. Size and HTTP status are not sufficient and
were not sufficient here: 10,049 ScienceDirect captures were 875 KB each,
returned HTTP 200, passed every structural check, and contained the sentence
*"There was a problem providing the content you requested"* instead of an
article.

The rule is *or* rather than *and*, and the reason is worth stating because it
looks like the weaker choice. Requiring both would discard 28,087 captures —
29.1% of the corpus — on a test that does not measure what it appears to. The
title check compares the record against the **page title**, and on a retraction
page the page title is "Retraction — 2012 — Pediatric Transplantation", or the
journal name, or a consent banner. None of that means the wrong document was
captured.

Asking the question the other way round — is the record's title present in the
extracted *text* — over 600 of those documents:

| title words found in the page text | documents | |
|---|---:|---:|
| 80% or more | 574 | 95.7% |
| 50–80% | 4 | 0.7% |
| 20–50% | 3 | 0.5% |
| under 20% | 19 | 3.2% |

96.3% carry at least half the title. Stage 2 agreed when this was measured on
2026-09-10, over the 27,238 DOI-only captures of the time: 27,090 (99.5%)
extracted some body text and 9,744 (35.8%) a full article of 3,000 characters
or more, which a wrong document would not.

**Every archive has now been checked individually.** An earlier version of
this file had to say that 70,168 were verified here and the other 18,851 —
the Elsevier TDM, PMC and merged SAGE material — were verified inside their own
collectors, with the evidence in code rather than in a file anyone could read.
`verify_archives.py` now runs across every stored capture - 96,514 of them,
including the IOP delivery, the campus-network batch and the Taylor & Francis

`archive_checks.csv` carries a verdict for each:

|---|---:|---:|

| DOI matches only | 28,087 | 29.1% |
| title matches only | 80 | 0.1% |

| a CDN error page served at the article's URL — refused | 9 | — |

Not one document lost its `usable` flag in that run, and no file named by the

the paper.

## Headline findings

**Of the 37,070 papers whose citation history is fully visible around their
retraction, 25,749 (69%) are cited after the retraction notice**, and 14,660
(40%) receive more citations after than before.

> **Do not report that 69% on its own.** The window restriction below is
> necessary, but it is not neutral: it selects a population, and the population
> it selects is recent, already-cited papers. Every one of the 12,322 papers
> that was never cited at all falls outside it, by construction — a paper with
> no citations has no `counts_by_year` for the window to cover. The two groups
> are not the same papers measured two ways:
>
> | | window covers retraction | it does not |
> |---|---|---|
> | papers | 37,070 | 25,797 |
> | never cited at all | **0** | 12,322 |
> | median citations | 7 | 1 |
> | retracted 2023 or later | 23,075 (62%) | 3,247 |
> | retracted before 2010 | **0** | 2,858 |
>
> So the sentence the 69% actually supports is *"of papers that were already
> being cited and were retracted recently, 69% pick up at least one more
> citation afterwards."* **Against all 62,867 papers the figure is 41.0%**, and
> that denominator is the one a reader assumes when the restriction is not
> stated.
>
> There is a second problem underneath the first, and it is not yet resolved.
> 62% of the restricted group was retracted in 2023 or later, so their "after"
> window is 2023–2026 — the years in which a recent paper accrues most of its
> citations anyway. For those papers "still cited after retraction" may be
> measuring the ordinary citation curve rather than any failure of the
> retraction to take effect. Establishing which requires comparing papers whose
> before and after windows are the same length; until that is done, treat 69%
> as a description of the corpus and not as a finding about retraction.

A further 12,726 papers are deliberately excluded from that comparison.
OpenAlex reports citations year by year for a **rolling recent window**, not the
full history: in this corpus the earliest year available clusters hard at 2012.
For a paper retracted before that window opens, the pre-retraction citations
simply are not in the data, `citations_before` is 0 by construction, and any
before/after comparison measures the window rather than the paper. 8,220 papers
would have been counted as "cited more after retraction" for that reason alone.

An earlier version of this file reported 37,888 (61%) and 6,692 across the whole
corpus. Those figures mixed papers with complete and truncated histories and
have been withdrawn.

**Public attention is rare and extremely concentrated.** 40,499 papers (64%)
have no Altmetric record at all — no news, no social media, nothing. Only 2,513
(4%) were covered by news. The top of that distribution is dominated by
COVID-era retractions and Wakefield's MMR paper, so a mean across the corpus
describes a paper that does not exist.

**905 papers are cited on Wikipedia**, in 2,154 articles across 11 editions.
English accounts for 549 of them — searching English alone understates
Wikipedia reach by roughly half.

## Read this before computing any citation percentage

`cited_by_count` is OpenAlex's total. `citations_before/during/after` come from
its per-year breakdown, which covers a **rolling recent window, not the full
history** — the field is documented that way and the data shows it: the earliest
year present clusters at 2012, and the widest span in this corpus is 15 years.

```
total citations            1,320,238
before + during + after     1,126,214
unassignable to a year        194,024   (15%)
```

13,071 papers have no per-year data at all. The missing citations are
predominantly *older* ones, which for most papers means pre-retraction.

So there are two defensible figures and they are not interchangeable:

* **39%** of datable citations fall after the retraction year
* **33%** of all citations do

**Before computing any before/after figure, restrict to papers whose window
covers the retraction.** The condition is: the earliest year in
`counts_by_year` is at or before `retraction_year`, and `cited_by_count` is not
greater than the sum of the yearly figures. 37,070 of 49,796 papers qualify.
Without that restriction the result is dominated by papers whose early
citations were never in the data.

Say which denominator you used in anything you publish.

## Inside `cs43_stage2_text.zip` — the extracted text

Stage 2 reads the article body and the retraction notice out of each verified
archive. **96,329 documents**, all at extractor version 14: 94,417 carry a body
and 86,209 carry a retraction notice. That is one row for every usable
document — Stage 1 and Stage 2 agree exactly, 96,329 = 96,329.

**`stage2_index.csv`** — 96,329 rows, one per document, **no body text**. What
was extracted, how well, from which host, in which capture format, under which
extractor version, and the character counts for abstract, body and notice. This
is the file to open first: it answers "how much text do we actually have, and
from whom" without downloading any of it.

**`stage2_notices.csv.gz`** — 86,209 rows. The retraction notices as extracted. This
is what Stage 3 compares the news and social coverage against, and it is the
half of the corpus that says *why* each paper was retracted.

> Version 9 had one notice of **450,619 characters** - a whole page taken as the
> notice - which broke Python's `csv.reader` (default field limit 131,072). The
> longest notice is now 37,341 characters, inside the limit. If you read these
> files with the `csv` module anyway, raising the limit costs nothing:
>
> ```python
> import csv, sys
> csv.field_size_limit(min(sys.maxsize, 2**31 - 1))
> ```
>
> Median notice length is 1,110 characters and the 99th percentile is 3,000.

`stage2_fulltext.jsonl.gz` (341 MB, article bodies included) is **not in this
zip** — see *Not here*. JSONL rather than CSV because article bodies contain
every delimiter a CSV could use.

Before using the text:

* **`extractor_version` must be 14 everywhere.** If a row says otherwise the
  file is stale. Versions 4 to 13 are kept frozen in the database as snapshots;
  one version number never covers two rule sets.

  What changed since the version 9 bundle is recorded in the project log
  (2026-09-15 to 2026-09-17; not published). Notices no longer carry cookie banners, site menus, citation
  widgets or navigation rails, and a page that is both article and notice keeps
  only the statement rather than the whole page.

  **Since the version 11 bundle of 2026-09-16:** where the page offers only a
  heading such as "Notice of Retraction: <title>", the statement is now taken
  from the body. That put the withdrawal statement into `notice` for 9,385
  documents, 8,318 of them IEEE and 711 IOP, and not one notice lost its
  statement: 77,323 notices now say the paper was withdrawn, against 67,938
  in version 11. The same change moved 6,264 IEEE-style pages from `full` to
  `notice-only`, which is the grade they should always have had - their body
  is the notice, not an article. Mathematical notation (`\nabla`, `\rho`,
  `\right`) is kept intact in all 521 bodies that carry it. **Recompute any
  count of notices or grades made from the version 11 files.**
* **`source_kind` and `shared_body_kind` say what a row is before you filter
  it.**
  * `teammate-tandf` marks the 2,558 Taylor & Francis pages a teammate collected
    through the University library's access. They can be reported apart, or
    removed as one unit.
  * `shared_body_kind` is empty when the body is the paper's own. Do **not**
    drop every row that has one from a notice analysis: `notice-replaces-article`
    pages carry a real notice.
* **`method` ending in `+pdf` means the body was read from a PDF.** IOP
  supplied each article as a JATS XML and a PDF; for 2,339 the XML was
  metadata only, so the text comes from the PDF. For a retraction notice's
  own DOI the PDF *is* the notice and goes to `notice`, never `body`.
* **A non-empty `body` is not always this paper's body.** 11,764 documents
  share their body with another document, and the reason is nearly always the
  publisher rather than the extractor:

  | | documents | what it is |
  |---|---|---|
  | `notice-replaces-article` | 10,655 | the publisher serves the retraction notice *in place of* the article |
  | `shared-body` | 779 | the same text filed under more than one DOI |
  | `bulk-notice` | 289 | one notice covering many papers at once |
  | `interface-not-article` | 41 | a cookie or access-interstitial page, not content |

  These are findings about how publishers handle retracted work, not defects to
  clean out. The `shared_bodies` table records which document is which.

* **IEEE is the largest single case and it does not look like one.** All 9,834
  IEEE documents have a non-empty `body`, so a completeness check on "is the
  body blank" passes them. The body is a ~1,000-character stub — the abstract
  field literally reads `Retracted.` — and its length barely varies (10th
  percentile 1,004 characters, 90th 1,122, against a median of 1,855 across
  all non-empty bodies).
  IEEE removes the article text and serves the notice; 9,373 of the 9,834 are
  flagged `notice-replaces-article` above. There is no open-access copy, no PMC
  copy and no pre-retraction Wayback capture to recover it from, so this is a
  result about the publisher, not a gap to fill. Since version 12 IEEE's own
  statement ("in violation of IEEE's Publication Principles") is in `notice`,
  so a reason analysis no longer has to read IEEE bodies. **Filter on body length or on
  `shared_bodies`, not on `body IS NOT NULL`.** 1,912 documents in the whole
  corpus have an empty body (1,912 of 96,329): 1,167 of them are IOP
  notices whose PDF was read into `notice` by design, leaving 745 with no text found.

## Before you load any of these

`original_doi_prefix` and `retraction_doi_prefix` look numeric but are **text**.
A plain `read_csv` turns `10.3390` into `10.339`, silently merging MDPI with a
different publisher — 82 prefixes covering 4,781 pages are affected, and three
pairs collide outright. Pass `dtypes.json`, or use `rwpipe.clean.read_clean()`.

## Known gaps

**PlumX is collected, and most of it should not be reported.** Access was
resolved with an institutional token from the library and all 62,867 papers were
asked on 2026-09-06/07; 59,172 have a record. An audit of the result found that
only one of its five columns survives scrutiny:

| column | usable? | why |
|---|---|---|
| `capture_count` | **yes** | reference-manager saves; non-zero on 95.9%, median 13 |
| `citation_count` | as a cross-check only | r=0.985 against OpenAlex — the same literature, not a second source. Also adds policy, patent and clinical-trial citations to scholarly ones. |
| `usage_count` | **no** | present on 0.7% of papers; 94.7% of the total belongs to 53 SciELO records whose payload is not per-article. A zero means the publisher sends PlumX no usage data, not that nobody read the paper. |
| `social_count` | **no** | Facebook only, and not per-article: four Taylor & Francis papers read 72,793 / 72,788 / 72,787 / 72,776 in the order we fetched them — one domain-level counter drifting between reads, filed under four DOIs. |
| `mention_count` | with care | news + blogs + Wikipedia references. Quoting it as "news" overstates the affected papers by 1.74x. |

**No PlumX number can be split at the retraction date.** The response carries no
date field of any kind — the payload was walked exhaustively and has 11 distinct
paths, none temporal. Every total is lifetime-cumulative. Measured against
OpenAlex, which can be split: for papers retracted less than two years before we
asked, the median share of a paper's attention falling after retraction is
**0.0%** (n=6,919), rising to 48.3% at five years or more. So for recent
retractions a PlumX lifetime total is a pre-retraction number, and reporting it
as continued attention would be wrong rather than merely imprecise.

`plumx.csv` is in `cs43_attention_and_access.zip` all the same, one row per
paper, because a number nobody can check is worse than a caveated one. Use
`capture_count` as it stands; treat every other column by the table above.
An earlier version of this file said the PlumX tables were not exported, while
the zip carried them.

**Wikipedia is a lower bound.** We search for the DOI string in article
wikitext. An article citing a paper by PMID or title alone is missed, as is a
citation that has since been edited away. Altmetric flags 3,174 papers as
Wikipedia-cited where our search confirms 905.

**Altmetric totals still have no time axis — the individual posts do.** The
summary endpoint (`altmetric.csv`, `score`, `news_count`, `social_count`)
reports totals to date and **cannot** be split before/after retraction. Do not
present an Altmetric total as post-retraction attention. Use
`altmetric_posts.csv.gz` for anything time-based: 1,437,967 mentions with a
date each, covering the 22,368 papers Altmetric has a record for.

**Social posts are IDs, not text.** Our Altmetric licence returns X/Twitter
post identifiers without the post content. News, blog and policy mentions do
carry a title and summary; 36,888 news mentions across 2,513 papers, 33,033 of
them with a summary.

## Not here

**The full text bodies.** `stage2_fulltext.jsonl.gz` is **341 MB** — over
GitHub's 100 MB per-file limit, so it cannot live in this folder. It is the
only file of the set that has to be fetched separately; ask for it directly.
The index and the notices are both in `cs43_stage2_text.zip` and between them
answer most questions without it.

**The page archives themselves** — 33.3 GB across 96,518 MHTML, HTML, JATS and
Elsevier XML files. Too large for git; shared separately, and **not
redistributable**: they are publisher copyright, and the Elsevier portion was
obtained under a text-and-data-mining licence granted for this project's
analysis only. `orig_file` and `notice_file` in the master table record each
archive's path. Use them through this repository, not by re-hosting them. The IOP files, and
the PDFs under `data/pdf/`, came under the University's TDM licence with IOP
via CAUL, on condition that local copies are deleted once processing is
finished; the PDFs are not in this folder or in any archive here.

**`archive_index.csv` is how you use it.** The files are named
`mhtml/<doi-prefix>/<xx>/<sha1>.mhtml.gz` — a layout that suits a crawler and
tells a person nothing. Handed the folder alone you can count the files and
learn nothing else. The index gives one row per file on disk, all 106,898 of
them, carrying `doi`, `publisher`, `journal`, `doc_role`, `host`,
`capture_format`, `usable`, the verification `verdict` and the byte size. Join
it to anything else on `doi`, or look a file up by `path`.

Two things to know before copying that folder:

* **Every referenced archive is present.** All 96,518 paths named in
  `documents.mhtml_path` resolve to a file that exists — checked, not assumed.
* **The folder holds more than the corpus, and that is deliberate.** 106,898
  `.gz` files are on disk; 10,380 of them (5.55 GB) have no database row.
  They are rejected Elsevier captures — 10,106 carry the `10.1016` prefix —
  containing `Subject: Redirecting` waypoints and the 539 KB ScienceDirect page
  reading *"There was a problem providing the content you requested"*. They are
  kept because they are the evidence that the refusals were real rather than a
  fault in our client, and a reviewer who wants to check that claim needs the
  files, not our summary of them. They appear in `archive_index.csv` with
  `in_database = 0`, `status = rejected`, and the URL each was saved from — so
  they are labelled rather than mixed in. Filter on `in_database = 1` for the
  corpus alone; the full folder is what gets shared.
